ADM Study Guide · Technical Screen Qs · Cursor Product Guide

Tactical Question Bank

More practical, persona-specific scenarios for how a CTO, CISO, or Staff Engineer interfaces with an ADM.

By Richard Reyes · Practice out loud · Calibrate your framing by audience

Section 1

CTO & VP of Engineering Conversations 6 Qs

Budget owners and org leaders. They care about velocity, headcount, retention, and competitive advantage — and they’ve heard a hundred pitches.

Q1
"We already gave everyone Copilot licenses six months ago. Usage is at 25%. Why would I spend political capital switching to something else?"
What makes this hard: You’re asking them to change direction without making them look wrong.

Don’t frame it as “Copilot failed.” Frame it as “your team is ready for the next step.” The 25% usage tells you a quarter of engineers are AI-receptive — those are your champions. The question is why the other 75% didn’t stick: autocomplete-only value, lack of codebase grounding, no structured onboarding, no team-level configuration.

Your pitch: run a 3‑week side-by-side with ~15 power users to test Agent mode + codebase Q&A on real work. If there’s no meaningful delta, no harm done. If there is, the CTO can present it internally as “evolving our AI strategy,” not reversing a prior decision.

Q2
"I need to justify this to my board. They want to see AI transforming our engineering org. What does that actually look like in 6 months?"
What makes this hard: Board narratives want clarity; engineering reality is incremental.

Give a 3‑act narrative: Foundation (pilot + activation), Momentum (wave expansion + champions), Transformation (cycle time down, onboarding faster, standards encoded in Rules). Then anchor with conservative ROI math: recovered capacity vs. annual license cost. The deliverable is a board-ready story, not a feature list.

Q3
"My VP of Platform says AI tools will create more technical debt, not less. She’s seen juniors ship AI-generated code they don’t understand. How do you respond?"
What makes this hard: It’s a legitimate risk backed by real behavior.

Validate the concern. Then propose guardrails: team Rules that encode architectural patterns, unchanged PR review discipline, start adoption with senior/staff engineers first, and use Plan Mode/checkpoints so approaches are reviewed before execution. Frame it as a maturity and enablement issue: with guardrails, Cursor can reduce debt by increasing consistency at scale.

Q4
"We’re in a hiring freeze. The roadmap assumes 20% more capacity. Can Cursor close that gap?"
What makes this hard: They want a quantifiable promise; overpromising kills trust.

Be precise: Cursor doesn’t create headcount; it recovers time from toil (boilerplate, tests, debugging, navigation, onboarding). Use typical self-reported savings (45–90 min/day) as a range, then propose a diagnostic to map their true bottlenecks. If bottlenecks are toil-heavy, Cursor can recover meaningful capacity; if bottlenecks are dependencies/infrastructure waits, Cursor helps but isn’t the whole answer.

Q5
"We’re acquiring a company next quarter. Their stack is Ruby on Rails; we’re Go and TypeScript. How does Cursor help with the integration?"
What makes this hard: Requires workflow thinking beyond standard rollout playbooks.

Use three wedges: codebase understanding (natural-language interrogation of unfamiliar code), translation/migration acceleration (Agent drafts cross-language ports with your patterns), and shared Rules for integration-layer consistency (API contracts, naming, error handling). M&A integration is a high-ROI use case because it shifts expensive senior time from reading to shipping.

Q6
"My engineering org is 400 people across 8 time zones. How do you support a rollout that isn’t US-centric?"
What makes this hard: Global rollouts require async enablement and regional champions.

Use a regional champion model, async-first enablement (recordings + docs), rotating office hours across time zones, localized quick wins by region/team workflow, and unified metrics with regional cut views. Don’t assume one culture or one working pattern.

Section 2

CISO & Security Leadership Conversations 5 Qs

Security leaders think in risk, data flow, access control, and auditability. Expect precise, technical questions and low tolerance for vagueness.

Q7
"Walk me through the exact data flow when a developer uses Cursor Chat. What leaves their machine, where does it go, who can see it, and how long is it retained?"
What makes this hard: You must be concrete about data handling.

Explain: local context gathering → local prompt construction → encrypted transit for inference → response rendered in-editor. With Privacy Mode, code is not stored on Cursor servers and not used for training. Where you’re not 100% certain, use the Honest Expert framework and commit to follow up with written documentation rather than guessing.

Q8
"We’re regulated under SOX and PCI-DSS. Our developers can’t use tools that touch production data or have access to our secrets management system. How does Cursor handle that?"
What makes this hard: Compliance teams care about boundaries and controls.

Clarify that Cursor operates in the IDE on source code; it doesn’t connect to prod DBs/vaults by default. MCP integrations are optional and admin-controlled. The main risk is secrets in source; mitigate with proper secrets hygiene plus Rules like “never include secrets in generated code.” Propose a joint security review mapping Cursor’s data handling to their control requirements.

Q9
"What happens if Cursor has a data breach? What’s in your incident response plan, and what’s our exposure?"
What makes this hard: They’re testing your composure in worst-case framing.

Don’t deflect. Separate (a) Cursor’s exposure surface (especially under Privacy Mode / zero retention), (b) model-provider exposure, and (c) incident response process (notification timelines and contractual commitments). Connect them with your security team for the formal plan in writing.

Q10
"Our policy is that no developer tool can send code outside our VPC. Full stop. What are your options?"
What makes this hard: It’s a hard constraint; you must respect it.

Discuss self-hosted options and/or routing to a customer-hosted model endpoint within the VPC (bring-your-own-model). If evaluation will take time, propose a scoped pilot on non-sensitive code while the self-hosted architecture is assessed.

Q11
"We had an audit that flagged Copilot telemetry being sent to Microsoft. How do I know Cursor isn’t doing the same thing?"
What makes this hard: They’re coming with a specific past scar.

Differentiate with Privacy Mode + org-level enforcement + audit-ready transparency. Invite independent validation during the pilot (e.g., security team network traffic analysis) rather than asking for blind trust.

Section 3

Staff Engineer & Technical Lead Conversations 5 Qs

Technical conscience of the org. They test whether this works in the messy reality of their codebase — not in a demo.

Q12
"I spend 60% of my time reviewing PRs from 8 developers. How does Cursor help me, specifically?"
What makes this hard: They want workflow impact, not marketing.

Three wedges: pre-review quality via Rules (more consistent PRs), Bugbot as a first-pass filter for mechanical issues, and codebase Q&A to regain context quickly during review. The reframe: reduce low-value review time so staff engineers focus on architecture and mentoring.

Q13
"Our codebase has 15 years of legacy code and a custom ORM nobody documented. Will Cursor hallucinate garbage?"
What makes this hard: Legacy + proprietary patterns are genuinely harder.

Be honest about the limitation, then explain how Cursor mitigates it: codebase indexing and retrieval pulls from the actual code, Rules encode local patterns, and Chat can help generate missing documentation. Propose an explicit pilot track that tests legacy areas early to calibrate trust.

Q14
"I’ve been writing Go for 12 years. The AI keeps suggesting patterns from 3 versions ago. How do I make it stop?"
What makes this hard: This is a common reason seniors become permanent skeptics.

Fix it with Rules (Go version + preferred libraries/patterns), @docs references to up-to-date docs, model selection, and consistent rejection of outdated patterns. The high-leverage move is turning one expert’s preferences into team-wide Rules.

Q15
"We have a 4M-line monorepo. Agent mode tries to change everything and creates chaos. What am I missing?"
What makes this hard: Scoping is everything at monorepo scale.

Use explicit scoping via @folder/@file, Rules that encode monorepo boundaries, and smaller, well-bounded tasks. Set expectations: monorepos push context management limits; configuration and scoping discipline are part of the rollout.

Q16
"My team writes Rust. AI tools are notoriously bad at Rust because of the borrow checker. Convince me."
What makes this hard: Rust’s type/ownership constraints are real.

Acknowledge it. Then reposition value: boilerplate/scaffolding, test scaffolding, codebase navigation Q&A, and documentation. Recommend starting with Tab + Chat and expanding only where it proves reliable.

Section 4

AI-Forward Teams — Maximizing Value 3 Qs

These teams already adopted. Now the conversation is about depth, scaling best practices, and moving from interactive assistance to workflow automation.

Q17
"We’re already heavy Cursor users — 90% weekly active. What’s the next level?"
What makes this hard: Requires optimization thinking, not adoption basics.

Move up the stack: Automations/Cloud Agents, MCP integrations (Linear/Sentry/etc.), advanced Rules that encode architecture decisions, shared prompt libraries, and metrics maturity (quality/cycle-time deltas, not just usage).

Q18
"Our most productive engineer uses Agent mode for everything. Should we make everyone work that way?"
What makes this hard: Universalizing one power user’s workflow usually backfires.

Don’t mandate. Capture her workflows (recordings/templates), run peer workshops, and create progressive challenges so others can adopt at their trust level. Power users succeed partly because they can evaluate output; less-experienced engineers may not.

Q19
"We want Cursor to enforce our architectural standards automatically. Not just suggest — enforce. Is that possible?"
What makes this hard: “Enforcement” requires layered controls.

Be precise: Rules strongly guide AI output but don’t prevent manual non-compliance. For enforcement, pair Rules with linters, CI checks, and automated review. Think proactive (Rules) + reactive (lint/CI) + human judgment (review).

Section 5

AI-Hesitant & Resistant Teams 5 Qs

Trust-building from zero: start small, prove safety, and let peer influence do more work than mandates.

Q20
"A respected Staff Engineer won’t use any tool that sends code to a third-party server. She’s also blocking adoption for 40 people. What do you do?"
What makes this hard: You can’t go around her without creating backlash.

Go through her: a 1:1 listening session, address concerns with specifics (Privacy Mode / self-hosted options), give her control to design guardrails for the pilot, and start with her lowest-risk use case. Turn a blocker into the architect of safe adoption.

Q21
"We banned AI tools after generated code introduced vulnerabilities. How do you reopen that conversation?"
What makes this hard: There’s organizational scar tissue and a real reason for the ban.

Don’t ask to reverse the ban; revisit the conditions for safe use. Show what’s changed (guardrails, Rules, admin controls, Privacy Mode). Propose a security-first, tightly scoped pilot with security-owned Rules and a post-pilot audit to validate outcomes.

Q22
"Half my team is in their 50s. They’ve written Java since 1.2. They think AI tools are a fad. What’s your play?"
What makes this hard: Patronizing veterans will permanently alienate them.

Lead with respect. Start with boring, low-friction wins (Tab for boilerplate), frame Rules as knowledge capture, and rely on peer influence rather than mandates. Avoid framing as “you’re behind.”

Q23
"Engineers fear AI tools will be used to measure productivity and replace them. How do you address that fear?"
What makes this hard: It’s existential, not technical.

Take it seriously. Clarify that adoption metrics should be used for org health (activation and enablement), not individual surveillance. Recommend leadership explicitly commit that AI usage won’t be used in performance reviews, and position the tool as an investment in developer experience, not monitoring.

Q24
"I lead embedded engineers writing C for IoT devices. We don’t use VS Code. Our build system is custom. Is Cursor relevant?"
What makes this hard: Not every workflow is a good fit; honesty matters.

Be candid: likely not Phase 1. Where it may help: documentation, test scaffolding, and companion scripting/CI work. Position as a later-wave candidate unless the team already works in VS Code-like environments.

Section 6

Mixed-Maturity Organizations 4 Qs

Most enterprises are mixed: some teams are AI-forward, some cautious, some resistant. Run coordinated rollouts with shared governance and team-specific entry points.

Q25
"Frontend wants Agent mode everywhere. Backend only wants Tab. Platform refuses AI. How do you run one rollout?"
What makes this hard: One-size-fits-all fails; total customization doesn’t scale.

Run three coordinated rollouts with a shared framework (admin controls, metrics dashboard, shared channel) and team-specific entry points. Use AI-forward teams as accelerators and internal proof, pragmatic teams as steady adopters, and resistant teams via 1:1 trust-building and lowest-risk use cases.

Q26
"We have 50 engineers in SF and 150 offshore contractors on a separate codebase. Should they get Cursor too?"
What makes this hard: Licensing, security boundaries, and rotation risk.

Weigh ROI (onboarding + consistency via Rules) against security policy and seat churn. Recommendation: include contractors in Phase 2 after the core pilot proves the playbook and security defines contractor-specific guardrails.

Q27
"Data science uses Jupyter. ML uses PyCharm. DevOps lives in the terminal. How does Cursor fit a polyglot tooling environment?"
What makes this hard: Not every workflow is IDE-centric; you must segment honestly.

Segment by workflow: notebooks are a strong fit; JetBrains users can be supported via ACP; terminal-centric teams benefit via infrastructure-as-code and CI/CD generation. Position Cursor as a multiplier where it fits, not a replacement for every tool.

Q28
"We’re an AI-first company. Our engineers are more technical than most customers. What does an ADM do for us?"
What makes this hard: The value is operational, not “teaching AI basics.”

Position the ADM as an operations multiplier: standardize Rules at scale, propagate best practices cross-team, produce exec-ready ROI reporting, maintain a tight product feedback loop, and make the best workflows reproducible via templates and enablement.

Section 7

How to Practice

These questions are meant to be practiced like real meetings — persona shifts, follow-ups, and time limits.

High-Impact Practice Drills
  1. Role-play by persona — Answer 3–4 questions in a row as if it’s one continuous meeting.
  2. Practice “I don’t know” moments — For security/data flow questions, practice the Honest Expert framework instead of guessing.
  3. Stack two personas — Practice pivoting between CTO framing and Staff Engineer framing mid-stream.
  4. Time box — 90 seconds for a direct question; 2 minutes max for a scenario.
  5. Record and listen — Listen for defensiveness, vagueness, or missing empathy.
  6. Adoption spectrum drill — Position the same feature three ways: AI-forward, pragmatic, resistant.
